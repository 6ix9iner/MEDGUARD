2.5 Conclusion of Literature Review
The literature review discusses the progress made in development of CDSS based on an evaluation of the technological changes that have occurred. These areas are alert fatigue, LLMs, RAG and autonomous agents; each area was evaluated during the literature review. However, the major takeaway from the literature is that despite the immense improvement in terms of technology, there is little translational success when it comes to developing safe and effective solutions. Alert fatigue is one of the basic concerns in relation to which contextually irrelevant alerts, too many false positives, and static rule-based logic continue to play crucial roles. While the introduction of selective alerts and policy changes have helped in mitigating alert burden in some settings, their limitations cannot be overlooked.
Modern developments in LLMs have made available some of the most effective reasoning and summarization tools that facilitate complex analysis of patient data and support clinical decisions. At the same time, it is evident from numerous studies that these models suffer from some of the essential problems, such as hallucinations, old information, opacity, and built-in biases, which pose significant barriers for their use in practice. Even though there is some progress in building agentic LLMs with superior capabilities in multiple-step reasoning and task execution, most evaluations are based on simulations and thus do not shed light on the actual efficacy of these solutions. The application of RAG as a mitigation technique has proven to be effective, dealing with hallucinations and outdated information by anchoring the responses provided by LLMs in credible external data. There is consistent evidence of gains in terms of improved accuracy, relevance of alerts, and errors, especially for applications that involve the administration of medications. However, it should be noted that RAG techniques are yet to be tested in actual clinical settings. The issues of ethical governance, bias amplification in retrieval systems, scaling of computations, and knowledge updates have not been adequately discussed. The primary challenge that connects all of the themes is that none of them have been validated using prospective clinical data collected from real world settings. The vast majority of work done within this space has utilized simulated data sets or hypothesized workflows. As such, it is difficult to determine how these systems will operate under true clinical use conditions (i.e., how clinicians interact with the system, the diversity of patients that may be treated, how different workflows are adapted to accommodate multiple treatment approaches, etc.), as well as how the system's operation will degrade over time due to various forms of "drift." Additionally, while many authors acknowledge and discuss ethical implications (e.g., performing bias audits, ensuring transparency, holding individuals accountable for system performance, etc.) related to AI-based systems in healthcare, few authors provide operational examples of how these can be implemented. The gap between what an AI application could do theoretically and what it is actually capable of delivering in a clinical environment is one of the biggest hurdles facing AI’s broader acceptance.

Collectively, it can be seen that none of the above approaches—traditional CDSS’s, stand-alone LLM’s, RAG frameworks and/or agent systems —are able to provide sufficient coverage of all the clinical, ethical and operational demands for providing a safe medication decision support system. It appears from this body of research, therefore, that what is needed are integrated hybrid models of decision support (to include agentic reasoning, retrieval based verification and explicit ethical oversight) to be tested in prospective clinical deployments as opposed to simulations conducted retrospectively.
It can be observed that in combination, all of the approaches mentioned above such as conventional CDSS, standalone LLMs, RAG, and also agents systems do not have the capability to address all of the needs required of a safe decision support system for medication prescription in clinical settings. It seems that from the body of the literature presented here that what is required is an integrative approach where hybrid decision support (including agents reasoning, retrieval-based verification, and ethical review) should be tested prospectively in clinical settings.
